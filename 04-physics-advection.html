6 Advection and RK4

The integrator is the engine. This chapter explains why DriftScope uses fourth-order Runge–Kutta, what its alternatives would look like, and how OceanParcels actually does the work.

6.1 The problem

We need to solve

\[ \frac{d\mathbf{X}}{dt} = \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}, t) \]

starting from \(\mathbf{X}(t_0) = \mathbf{X}_0\). The right-hand side is known only at discrete grid points and discrete time steps — we have a NetCDF, not an analytic function. So:

  • We need a way to evaluate \(\mathbf{u}\) at arbitrary \((\mathbf{x}, t)\) in space–time. That is interpolation.
  • We need a way to step \(\mathbf{X}\) forward in time using \(\mathbf{u}\). That is time integration.

These two pieces are mostly orthogonal. Bad interpolation can ruin a good integrator (and vice versa). DriftScope uses bilinear-in-space + linear-in-time interpolation, and RK4 in time. Let’s see why each.

6.2 Why not just Euler?

The simplest integrator is forward Euler:

\[ \mathbf{X}_{n+1} = \mathbf{X}_n + \Delta t \cdot \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}_n, t_n) \tag{6.1}\]

Evaluate the velocity at where you are now, push the particle that direction for \(\Delta t\). One velocity probe per step, dead simple.

It is also wrong, in a specific way. Euler has first-order local truncation error: per step, you accumulate error \(\propto \Delta t^2\), and over a fixed total time the global error is \(\propto \Delta t\). Halve the time step, halve the error. This converges, but slowly. For a 10-day simulation in a shear-rich field, you’d need \(\Delta t < 5\) minutes to get a usable answer. That’s \(\sim 3000\) velocity probes per particle.

Worse, Euler is unstable in any rotational flow: a particle in a circular eddy spirals outward instead of orbiting. After one synthetic eddy period, the trajectory is unrecognizable. Bad.

6.3 RK4: four probes, fourth-order accuracy

Runge–Kutta 4 is the workhorse compromise between accuracy and computational cost. The recipe:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{k}_1 &= \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}_n, t_n) \\ \mathbf{k}_2 &= \mathbf{u}\!\left(\mathbf{X}_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf{k}_1,\; t_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\right) \\ \mathbf{k}_3 &= \mathbf{u}\!\left(\mathbf{X}_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf{k}_2,\; t_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\right) \\ \mathbf{k}_4 &= \mathbf{u}\!\left(\mathbf{X}_n + \Delta t\,\mathbf{k}_3,\; t_n + \Delta t\right) \\ \mathbf{X}_{n+1} &= \mathbf{X}_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{6}(\mathbf{k}_1 + 2\mathbf{k}_2 + 2\mathbf{k}_3 + \mathbf{k}_4) \end{aligned} \tag{6.2}\]

In words: probe the velocity at the start of the step (\(\mathbf{k}_1\)), then at the midpoint using \(\mathbf{k}_1\) as the slope (\(\mathbf{k}_2\)), then again at the midpoint using the better \(\mathbf{k}_2\) as the slope (\(\mathbf{k}_3\)), then at the endpoint using \(\mathbf{k}_3\) (\(\mathbf{k}_4\)). Take a weighted average — endpoints get weight 1, midpoints get weight 2 — and that is the slope for the step.

Costs four velocity probes per step. Pays back as \(O(\Delta t^4)\) local error and \(O(\Delta t^4)\) global error over fixed time. Halving \(\Delta t\) cuts error by \(16\times\). RK4 with \(\Delta t = 30\) min beats Euler with \(\Delta t = 1\) min in both accuracy and cost.

In a steady linear shear, RK4 also conserves energy to machine precision — no spurious spiraling.

Tip

The “Magic of RK4” sounds suspicious. Where does the fourth-order accuracy come from? Each midpoint probe captures one more term of the Taylor expansion of \(\mathbf{u}(t)\), and the weighted average is hand-tuned to cancel error terms up to fourth order. RK4 is the minimum-cost explicit scheme that achieves order 4 — there is no order-4 scheme with three or fewer evaluations. RK5 needs six. RK6 needs seven. Past four, the cost grows faster than the accuracy gain for typical ocean problems, which is why RK4 has been the default in every Lagrangian framework since the 1980s.

6.4 The Parcels implementation

OceanParcels (the framework DriftScope uses) ships a JIT-compiled AdvectionRK4 kernel. We use it directly:

from parcels import AdvectionRK4   # in driftscope/simulate.py:191

The kernel is just Eq. 6.2 written in a small DSL that Parcels JITs to C. For reference, the equivalent custom kernel — which we use when we want to gracefully delete particles that hit land — lives in driftscope/kernels.py:50:

def AdvectionRK4_Land(particle, fieldset, time):
    (u1, v1) = fieldset.UV[particle]
    lon1 = particle.lon + u1 * 0.5 * particle.dt
    lat1 = particle.lat + v1 * 0.5 * particle.dt

    (u2, v2) = fieldset.UV[time + 0.5 * particle.dt, particle.depth, lat1, lon1, particle]
    lon2 = particle.lon + u2 * 0.5 * particle.dt
    lat2 = particle.lat + v2 * 0.5 * particle.dt

    (u3, v3) = fieldset.UV[time + 0.5 * particle.dt, particle.depth, lat2, lon2, particle]
    lon3 = particle.lon + u3 * particle.dt
    lat3 = particle.lat + v3 * particle.dt

    (u4, v4) = fieldset.UV[time + particle.dt, particle.depth, lat3, lon3, particle]

    particle_dlon += (u1 + 2 * u2 + 2 * u3 + u4) / 6.0 * particle.dt
    particle_dlat += (v1 + 2 * v2 + 2 * v3 + v4) / 6.0 * particle.dt

Read line by line — it is exactly Eq. 6.2 in code. The four UV[...] reads are the four velocity probes. The final two lines accumulate the position update; Parcels then writes them to particle.lon, particle.lat after the kernel chain finishes.

Note

particle_dlon and particle_dlat are magic identifiers that Parcels recognizes. You don’t declare them; the JIT compiler resolves them to private accumulators per particle. Ditto particle_ddepth for the vertical component (used in Chapter 9). The noqa: F821 annotations in the source silence linters that don’t know about this.

6.5 Choosing \(\Delta t\)

DriftScope’s default integration step is 30 minutes (SimConfig.dt_minutes = 30, in driftscope/config.py). This is small enough that RK4 stays well below 1 km position error per day in typical Bay of Bengal flows (peak speed ~1 m/s). Smaller is wasteful; larger and you start to see the trajectory cross multiple grid cells per step, which the linear interpolation can’t represent honestly.

Two regimes flip that default:

  • Tides on. When you add tidal forcing the field has hourly variability with peak speeds of 1–2 m/s in the Sundarbans. The combined field is re-gridded onto a 1-hour time axis (see driftscope/simulate.py:91), and the 30-minute step is then short enough that you see the M2 oscillation.
  • Output cadence. Distinct from the integration step: you don’t have to save every step. Default is output_minutes = 180 (every 3 h), which gives 81 saved positions over 10 days — plenty for plotting, animations, and statistics. The integration is the cost; saving is essentially free.
Tip

The CFL number, casually. A rule of thumb for stability: \(u_\text{max} \Delta t / \Delta x < 1\). With \(u_\text{max} \approx 1\) m/s and CMEMS \(\Delta x \approx 8000\) m, \(\Delta t < 8000\) s (\(\approx 2\) h) keeps you safe. Our 30-minute step has plenty of headroom; doubling to 1 hour would still be stable for currents-only runs. In tidal regimes the grid is unchanged but \(u_\text{max}\) doubles — still safe at 30 min.

6.6 Land, NaN, and out-of-bounds

A particle that wanders into a coast cell samples a NaN. Parcels’ default is to raise. That makes coastal regions unworkable. DriftScope takes a gentler approach: a tiny extra kernel DeleteOOB (simulate.py:381) runs after AdvectionRK4 and removes any particle that has gone out of bounds.

def DeleteOOB(particle, fieldset, time):
    if particle.state == StatusCode.ErrorOutOfBounds:
        particle.delete()

The result is what you want: particles that drift toward shore disappear (beached) instead of crashing the whole run. The trajectory file just has fewer rows in later time steps; pandas/xarray handle this transparently.

6.7 Summary

  • DriftScope integrates Eq. 5.2 with RK4 at 30-minute steps.
  • Trajectories are bilinearly interpolated in space, linearly in time. Both are configurable but rarely worth changing.
  • Particles are JIT-compiled to C — runtime is dominated by NetCDF I/O, not the integrator.
  • DeleteOOB turns out-of-bounds errors into clean particle deletion, making coastal simulations practical.
