9 Settling — Stokes’ law for SPM

A real sediment grain isn’t a tracer that follows the flow — it falls through it. Slowly. The vertical fall speed sets where it ends up relative to a perfect-tracer particle. This chapter derives the settling velocity, sites it in the DriftScope kernel chain, and explains the range of validity.

9.1 Stokes’ law

A small spherical particle of density \(\rho_p\) and diameter \(d\) falling through a fluid of density \(\rho_w\) and viscosity \(\mu\) has terminal velocity (downward positive):

\[ w_s \;=\; \frac{(\rho_p - \rho_w)\,g\,d^2}{18\,\mu} \;=\; \frac{2}{9}\,\frac{(\rho_p - \rho_w)\,g\,r^2}{\mu} \tag{9.1}\]

with \(r = d/2\). Derived by Stokes (1851) by balancing buoyancy (\(-\rho_w g V\)), gravity (\(\rho_p g V\)), and the viscous drag on a sphere at low Reynolds number (\(6\pi \mu r u\)). Set the net force to zero and solve for \(u\) — that’s \(w_s\).

The DriftScope helper function lives at driftscope/kernels.py:27:

def stokes_settling_velocity(diameter_um, particle_density_kg_m3=2650.0,
                             water_density_kg_m3=1025.0,
                             viscosity_pa_s=1.002e-3):
    r = (diameter_um * 1e-6) / 2.0
    return ((2.0 / 9.0) *
            (particle_density_kg_m3 - water_density_kg_m3) /
            viscosity_pa_s * GRAVITY * r * r)
Tip

Why \(r^2\)? The drag force scales linearly with size (\(6\pi\mu r u\)), but the gravitational drive scales with volume (\(\rho V \propto r^3\)). Volume divided by drag gives \(r^2\). Larger particles fall much faster. A \(50\,\mu\)m grain falls \(25\times\) faster than a \(10\,\mu\)m grain.

9.2 Magnitudes for the Sundarbans

Plug in the Bay of Bengal SPM size distribution:

Class \(d\) \(w_s\) \(w_s\) in m/day
Clay (fine) 1 µm \(9 \times 10^{-7}\) m/s 0.08
Clay (med) 2 µm \(4 \times 10^{-6}\) m/s 0.3
Silt (fine) 5 µm \(2 \times 10^{-5}\) m/s 1.9
Silt (10 µm) 10 µm \(9 \times 10^{-5}\) m/s 7.6
Silt (coarse) 50 µm \(2.2 \times 10^{-3}\) m/s 190
Fine sand 100 µm \(9 \times 10^{-3}\) m/s 770

DriftScope’s default is 10 µm silt-class quartz at \(\rho_p = 2650\) kg/m³. This grain falls ~8 m/day — fast enough that in a 10-day run, particles released at the surface end up at the seabed of a typical 50–80 m shelf. Slow enough that horizontal drift over 10 days (10 km/day at 0.1 m/s) is comparable to vertical, so dispersion happens in 3D, not 2D.

9.3 When Stokes’ law breaks

Eq. 9.1 assumes:

  1. Stokes flow around the sphere — particle Reynolds number \(Re_p = d\,w_s\,\rho_w/\mu < 0.5\).
  2. Spherical particles.
  3. Single particle in dilute suspension (no neighbors).
  4. Newtonian fluid at constant viscosity.

For SPM in seawater: assumption 1 holds up to \(d \approx 80\) µm. Beyond that, the wake separates and Stokes’ law overpredicts \(w_s\).

For real estuarine sediment: assumption 2 fails badly. Clay particles are plates, silt grains are angular, and flocculation glues them into porous flocs that can be 10–100 µm aggregates of 1 µm primary particles. A floc settles slower than a single \(10\,\mu\)m grain because its effective density (water-saturated mineral) is much lower than \(\rho_p\). Real-world \(w_s\) for “10 µm silt” in a turbid estuary is often 1–3 m/day, not 8.

Warning

The settling-velocity calibration is the single largest uncertainty in SPM transport modeling, and it varies by season, salinity, and SPM concentration itself. DriftScope’s default is “single mineral grain at mid-shelf salinity” — fine for a teaching tutorial; not fine for a calibrated regulatory model.

DriftScope makes the assumption explicit by exposing settling_diameter_um and settling_density_kg_m3 in SimConfig:

@dataclass
class SimConfig:
    ...
    include_settling: bool = False
    settling_diameter_um: float = 10.0
    settling_density_kg_m3: float = 2650.0

9.4 Why settling is a kernel, not a field

Stokes drift, tides, and windage are added at the data layer — they’re spatial fields you sum into one combined NetCDF. Settling is different: it is a per-particle velocity that depends on the particle’s own properties (size, density). Two particles at the same location can have different settling velocities. So it has to live in a kernel — JIT-compiled code that runs per-particle inside the integration loop.

driftscope/kernels.py:86:

def Settling(particle, fieldset, time):
    """Stokes settling for SPM. Requires per-particle `w_settle` (m/s, +down)."""
    particle_ddepth += particle.w_settle * particle.dt

Three lines, one of them a comment. The accumulator particle_ddepth is Parcels’ magic identifier for “increment the depth by this much” — same pattern as particle_dlon, particle_dlat from Chapter 6.

The per-particle attribute w_settle is set up in simulate.py:235 when settling is enabled:

class SedimentParticle(JITParticle):
    w_settle = Variable("w_settle", dtype=np.float32, initial=w_s)
ParticleClass = SedimentParticle

Every particle gets the same w_s initially, but you could subclass SedimentParticle to give each particle a different size — trivially extending the framework to handle a poly-disperse SPM population. We don’t do that yet; one constant for now.

9.5 Kernel chain order

The kernel chain runs once per integration step, in order:

# simulate.py:385
kernels = [AdvectionRK4]
if use_settling: kernels.append(Settling)
if use_brownian: kernels.append(BrownianMotion2D)
kernels.append(DeleteOOB)

Order matters. AdvectionRK4 first computes the horizontal step using the combined Eulerian velocity. Then Settling adds the vertical step. Then BrownianMotion2D adds horizontal noise. Then DeleteOOB cleans up. If you swapped settling and Brownian, the answer is the same to within \(\Delta t^2\) (operator-splitting error), so order between physics kernels mostly doesn’t matter — but we keep advection first by convention because it’s the primary motion.

9.6 What settling does to the trajectory

A particle that settles eventually leaves the surface velocity field and starts experiencing whatever subsurface velocities the model resolves. For CMEMS/HYCOM, “subsurface” means the next vertical level (depth ~5 m or so depending on dataset).

In practice:

  • Day 0: particle at \(z = 0\), surface drift dominates.
  • Day 1: \(z \approx -8\) m (default 10-µm silt). Still in the surface mixed layer in monsoon BoB; little change in horizontal velocity.
  • Day 5: \(z \approx -38\) m. Below the seasonal thermocline in pre-monsoon; horizontal velocity is much weaker, ~0.05 m/s.
  • Day 10: \(z \approx -76\) m. Mid-shelf or deeper. Particle has effectively decoupled from surface variability.

This means settled particles cluster under their release point more than perfect tracers do. The accumulation pattern in a coupled SPM run shows this: a higher concentration directly below the river mouth and along the shelf, less spread offshore than a no-settling run.

Tip

Compare with and without settling. Run the same simulation twice with include_settling = True/False and overlay the final-position scatter. The difference is the SPM-specific signal — what makes sediment behave differently from a passive tracer. See the validation tutorial for the plotting code.

9.7 Summary

  • Settling velocity is given by Stokes’ law for small spherical particles in laminar flow, Eq. 9.1.
  • For 10 µm silt-class quartz, \(w_s \approx 8\) m/day — meaningful over a 10-day simulation.
  • Real estuarine SPM forms flocs; effective \(w_s\) is reduced by 3–5×. DriftScope doesn’t model flocculation but exposes the settling parameters so a student can experiment.
  • Settling lives in a per-particle kernel rather than a velocity field, because it depends on particle properties (size, density).
  • The kernel chain runs AdvectionRK4 → Settling → BrownianMotion2D → DeleteOOB per step.
