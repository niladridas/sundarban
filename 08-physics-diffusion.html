10 Sub-grid diffusion — Brownian motion

The Eulerian velocity field is smooth at the model grid scale. The real ocean is not. Eddies smaller than 1/12° (CMEMS) or 1/25° (HYCOM) are unresolved — they are real flow, but they don’t appear in the data. This chapter explains how DriftScope parameterizes these unresolved eddies as a stochastic random walk, and why that choice is conservative but honest.

10.1 The unresolved-eddy problem

Run a noiseless DriftScope simulation against a real Global Drifter Program trajectory released at the same point. After a few days, the simulated and real drifters separate. The drifter’s trajectory has scale-rich wiggles on time scales of hours and length scales of 1–10 km. The simulated trajectory is smooth.

Why? Because the drifter is in a real eddy field. CMEMS at 1/12° smooths across submesoscale eddies (1–10 km) and chops the inertial-period band out of the daily mean. So a simulated trajectory misses two things:

  1. Daily-mean filtering: inertial oscillations and sub-daily variance.
  2. Spatial resolution: submesoscale eddies and fronts.

The combined effect is that the model is missing \(O(0.1\)–\(1)\) m/s of short-time, small-scale variability. Over 10 days at 0.3 m/s of unresolved drift, that’s a position error of ~250 km in any direction — roughly what DriftScope’s drifter-validation chapter shows for CMEMS-only runs.

10.2 The simplest fix: random walk

A random walk added to the trajectory parameterizes the unresolved variability without claiming to model it explicitly. The mathematical object is a 2D Wiener process:

\[ d\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{u}\,dt + \sqrt{2 K_h}\,d\mathbf{W} \tag{10.1}\]

where \(d\mathbf{W} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}\,dt)\) — two independent unit-variance Gaussian increments per step. \(K_h\) is the horizontal eddy diffusivity (m²/s) — the strength of the random forcing.

The discrete version DriftScope uses, per timestep:

\[ \Delta x = \sqrt{2 K_h |\Delta t|} \cdot \mathcal{N}(0, 1), \qquad \Delta y = \sqrt{2 K_h |\Delta t|} \cdot \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \tag{10.2}\]

This adds a Gaussian-distributed kick to the particle’s position every step, with standard deviation \(\sigma = \sqrt{2 K_h |\Delta t|}\).

driftscope/kernels.py:114:

def BrownianMotion2D(particle, fieldset, time):
    rx = ParcelsRandom.normalvariate(0.0, 1.0)
    ry = ParcelsRandom.normalvariate(0.0, 1.0)
    sigma = math.sqrt(2.0 * fieldset.Kh * math.fabs(particle.dt))
    dx_m = sigma * rx
    dy_m = sigma * ry
    deg_per_m_lon = 1.0 / (111320.0 * math.cos(particle.lat * math.pi / 180.0))
    particle_dlat += dy_m / 110574.0
    particle_dlon += dx_m * deg_per_m_lon
Tip

The unit conversion at the bottom. Brownian draws are in meters; the particle position is in degrees. The conversion uses 1° lat = 110,574 m and 1° lon = \(111,\!320 \cdot \cos(\varphi)\) m. JIT compilation can’t capture module-level Python constants, which is why the numbers are hardcoded inline — read driftscope/kernels.py:111 for the gotcha note.

10.3 How big is \(K_h\)?

There is a literature on this; Phillips (1977) chapter 6 is the canonical discussion. Rough magnitudes:

\(K_h\) (m²/s) Regime Drift RMS spread per day
1 Coastal sheltered, weak eddies 0.4 km
10 Open shelf, low eddy energy 1.3 km
100 DriftScope default; open BoB 4.2 km
1000 Strong eddy field (Gulf Stream) 13 km
10000 Large-scale Atlantic gyres 42 km

The default \(K_h = 100\) m²/s is a Bay of Bengal-tuned number. It comes from the drifter validation: CMEMS-only runs miss an observed track by ~150 km at day 22, suggesting an unresolved-velocity scale of \(\sim 0.08\) m/s. Set \(K_h\) such that one day’s RMS spread is ~7 km (matches the observed scatter), which gives \(K_h \approx 280\) m²/s. We rounded to 100 to be conservative — undershooting the random walk leaves room for the deterministic model error, which we don’t want to hide behind random forcing.

Warning

Brownian is a parameterization, not physics. It says: “I don’t know what the unresolved velocities are, but on average they should add 4 km of RMS scatter per day.” It does not tell you about coherent submesoscale features (filaments, fronts), only about the mean energy of unresolved motion. The figure inspired by GOFLOW (Chapter 20) shows the real submesoscale field — adding \(K_h\) doesn’t recreate it; it just distributes the typical scatter.

10.4 Diffusion vs dispersion

Two related concepts that students conflate:

  • Diffusion is the spread of an ensemble — many particles released at the same point. The cloud broadens as \(\sigma(t) = \sqrt{2 K_h t}\).
  • Dispersion is the spread induced by the mean shear — different particles at slightly different starting points end up far apart because they sample different velocities along the way. This happens without any random forcing.

In the Bay of Bengal, dispersion typically dominates diffusion at scales larger than 10 km. A 500-particle cloud released in a 10-km disk will spread to 100 km in 10 days mostly from shear, and the Brownian random walk adds only ~13 km on top.

This is why DriftScope’s dispersion plots (Chapter 18) look noisy even with \(K_h = 0\): the deterministic flow is doing most of the work.

10.5 Reproducibility

Because Brownian draws are random, two runs with the same configuration give different answers. DriftScope passes a fixed seed to run_simulation() (default 42). The seed initializes both numpy’s RNG (used to place initial particles) and Parcels’ internal RNG (used inside JIT kernels). With the same seed, the run is bit-exact reproducible.

For ensemble work — running the same setup with many seeds to characterize the noise — pass different seeds and aggregate the results. The seasonal dispersion script does this; see Chapter 21.

10.6 When to turn it off

For exploring the deterministic flow (which CMEMS or HYCOM features the model resolves, where eddies are, what the dominant trajectories look like) — turn Brownian off. Set include_brownian = False in SimConfig.

For operational drift forecasting (where will the spill go in 5 days, within what cone of uncertainty) — leave it on. The cone of uncertainty is what Brownian gives you.

A run with Brownian on but small \(K_h\) (\(1\) m²/s) is also a useful “smoothness check”: real CMEMS simulations are eddy-poor enough that you should be able to tell if your trajectory is plausibly noisy or is just plain wrong.

10.7 Summary

  • Resolved currents miss inertial-band and submesoscale variability.
  • DriftScope parameterizes this as a 2D Brownian random walk with diffusivity \(K_h\) (default 100 m²/s for the Bay of Bengal).
  • Implemented as a Parcels kernel after AdvectionRK4 — see driftscope/kernels.py:114.
  • It’s a statistical parameterization — it adds the right amount of scatter, not the right patterns. For the right patterns you need higher resolution data (Module 6 — GOFLOW).
