7  Stokes drift, tides, and windage

8 Stokes drift, tides, and windage

CMEMS gives you “currents” but not all of them. Three additional velocity contributions matter for surface objects, especially in shallow shelves and during energetic weather:

  1. Stokes drift — the second-order mean flow induced by surface waves.
  2. Tides — the dominant signal in shelf seas, filtered out of CMEMS by design.
  3. Windage — direct drag on the air-exposed fraction of an object.

DriftScope adds all three at the data layer: each has its own NetCDF, each is interpolated to the currents grid, all are summed before the integrator runs. driftscope/simulate.py:47’s combine_forcings() is where this happens. The architectural reason to do it at data-layer (not in a kernel) is that interpolation and summation are cheap, parallelizable, and don’t need to run inside the JIT-compiled integration loop.

8.1 Stokes drift

A pure linear wave looks like a sinusoidal up-and-down at every point — nothing should drift. But surface waves are not exactly linear. To second order in wave amplitude, a fluid parcel under the waves experiences a small mean displacement in the direction the waves are going. This is Stokes drift, derived by Stokes (1847).

For a single sinusoid of amplitude \(a\), period \(T\), wavenumber \(k = 2\pi/L\):

\[ U_s(z) \;=\; \omega k a^2\, e^{2 k z} \;=\; \frac{(2\pi)^2}{T^2} \cdot \frac{2\pi}{L} \cdot a^2 \cdot e^{2 k z} \tag{8.1}\]

with \(z = 0\) at the surface and \(z < 0\) below. The drift falls off exponentially below the surface; for a 10-second swell (\(L \approx 156\) m), the e-folding scale is about 12 m.

For a broadband wave field, the surface (\(z = 0\)) Stokes drift is

\[ U_s \;\approx\; \frac{(2\pi)^3 H_s^2}{8\,g\,T_m^3} \tag{8.2}\]

where \(H_s\) is the significant wave height and \(T_m\) is the mean wave period. This is the deep-water surface formula DriftScope uses; see driftscope/stokes.py:56:

def stokes_from_waves(swh, mwp, mwd):
    magnitude = (2 * np.pi) ** 3 * swh ** 2 / (8 * G * mwp ** 3)
    theta = np.deg2rad(270.0 - mwd)
    ust = magnitude * np.cos(theta)
    vst = magnitude * np.sin(theta)
    ...
Tip

The angle conversion is a classic gotcha. ERA5’s mwd is the meteorological convention: the direction the waves come from, in degrees clockwise from North. We need the math angle: where they’re going, in radians counterclockwise from East. The transformation is math_angle = 270° − mwd. If you forget the sign, your particles drift the wrong way for a few weeks before someone notices.

8.1.1 Magnitudes in the Bay of Bengal

Plug in monsoon-season values: \(H_s \approx 2.5\) m, \(T_m \approx 8\) s.

\[ U_s \approx \frac{(2\pi)^3 \cdot 6.25}{8 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 512} \approx 0.038 \text{ m/s} \]

So \(\sim 4\) cm/s during peak monsoon — small compared to the 0.2–0.5 m/s typical CMEMS surface currents, but not negligible over a 10-day simulation. Pre-monsoon and post-monsoon values are 1–2 cm/s.

8.1.2 Why a coefficient

The factor \((2\pi)^3 / (8g)\) in Eq. 8.2 assumes a narrow-band spectrum. Real wave spectra have spread, and different conventions assign different prefactors. OpenDrift uses \(\sim 8\pi^3 / g\), which is roughly \(8\times\) larger than ours. DriftScope exposes a stokes_scale knob in SimConfig so a student can experiment with this prefactor. We default to 1.0 (narrow-band textbook formula) and document this choice in the validation chapter.

8.2 Tides

Tidal currents are deterministic. Given a location and a moment in time, a calibrated harmonic model (TPXO9, FES2014, OTIS) tells you the barotropic \((u_\text{tide}, v_\text{tide})\) exactly. CMEMS GLORYS filters tides out during data assimilation — it would otherwise need to be assimilating 6-hourly tide-gauge data, which it doesn’t. So if you want tides, you have to add them.

In the Sundarbans, tides dominate everything else. Spring tides reach 1–1.5 m/s. CMEMS surface currents in the same place are 0.2–0.4 m/s (monsoon residual), 0.05–0.1 m/s (dry season). A particle simulation without tides in a delta region is physically wrong.

8.2.1 Harmonic prediction in 200 words

A tide model is a sum of sinusoids:

\[ u_\text{tide}(\mathbf{x}, t) = \sum_n A_n(\mathbf{x}) \cos\!\left[\omega_n t - \phi_n(\mathbf{x})\right] \]

where \(\omega_n\) and \(\phi_n\) are the frequency and phase of constituent \(n\). The big ones are \(M_2\) (12.42 h, principal lunar semidiurnal), \(S_2\) (12.00 h, principal solar semidiurnal), \(K_1\) (23.93 h, lunisolar diurnal), and \(O_1\) (25.82 h, principal lunar diurnal). TPXO9-atlas-v5 has 13 constituents on a 1/30° global grid.

The amplitude \(A_n(\mathbf{x})\) and phase \(\phi_n(\mathbf{x})\) are fitted once, by inverse modeling against tide gauges and altimetry Egbert & Erofeeva (2002). Prediction is then trivial: evaluate the sum.

DriftScope wraps this with pyTMD — see driftscope/tides.py:121:

res = pyTMD.compute.tide_currents(
    xx, yy, tt, component="u",
    directory=str(model_dir),
    model="TPXO9-atlas-v5-nc",
    epoch=(2000, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0),
    type="drift",
    standard="UTC",
    crs=4326,
    method="linear",
    extrapolate=False,
    crop=True,
    bounds=bounds, buffer=1.0,
)

The output is hourly \(u_\text{tide}\) and \(v_\text{tide}\) on the currents grid — see Chapter 13 for the data-side details and the seven pyTMD 3.x quirks the project navigated.

Tip

Tide-aware time grid. When you turn on tides, the simulation’s combined forcing NetCDF gets re-gridded onto the tides’ hourly time axis (see simulate.py:91). The currents (daily) are forward-filled to each hour. This preserves the full \(M_2\) oscillation that would otherwise be averaged away if we kept the daily grid.

8.3 Windage

Floating objects with parts above the waterline get pushed by the wind in addition to the water. The standard parameterization is empirical:

\[ \mathbf{u}_\text{windage} = \alpha\,\mathbf{u}_{10} \tag{8.3}\]

where \(\mathbf{u}_{10}\) is the 10 m wind and \(\alpha\) is a small empirical windage coefficient that depends on the air-to-water profile of the object:

\(\alpha\) Object class
0.01 Icebergs (mostly underwater)
0.02 Persons in water; sediment-laden water
0.02–0.04 Plastic debris (depends on shape)
0.03 DriftScope default; reasonable for general “buoyant material”
0.035 Oil slicks

The number is small but the wind is fast. Monsoon winds in the Bay of Bengal hit 10 m/s easily, so \(\alpha \mathbf{u}_{10}\) is 0.2–0.4 m/s — a big chunk of the surface velocity, comparable to or exceeding \(\mathbf{u}_\text{currents}\).

Warning

Don’t double-count Ekman. CMEMS already represents wind-driven Ekman currents in its surface flow (the model assimilates wind forcing). Windage is the additional direct drag on the emergent fraction of the object, on top of CMEMS + Stokes + tides. Don’t add it for a fully submerged particle (e.g. SPM at 1 m depth).

In code, windage is one numpy multiplication during the data-layer combine step (simulate.py:129):

u10 = wnd["u10"].interp({...}, time=time_grid).fillna(0.0) * windage_coeff
v10 = wnd["v10"].interp({...}, time=time_grid).fillna(0.0) * windage_coeff
uo = uo + u10
vo = vo + v10

That’s it. There’s no kernel for windage — once you’ve combined the velocity fields, the integrator doesn’t need to know which term came from which physics.

8.4 Putting them together

When you run a coupled simulation:

python -m driftscope.simulate \
    --stokes data/waves/<file>.nc \
    --tides data/tides/<file>.nc \
    --winds data/winds/<file>.nc \
    --windage 0.02

combine_forcings() produces a single combined NetCDF. The integrator runs on that. You can inspect the combined field by opening it directly with xarray — it has the same uo, vo schema as the raw CMEMS file.

The cache filename encodes which forcings are active:

sundarbans_delta_2026-04-17_2026-05-01__stokes1.0_tides_wind2.0pct.nc

so different combos don’t collide and reruns are idempotent.

8.5 Sanity check: which term dominates where?

Rough Bay-of-Bengal magnitudes during pre-monsoon:

Term Open ocean Shelf (50 m) Delta (10 m)
Currents 0.2–0.4 m/s 0.1–0.3 m/s 0.1–0.3 m/s
Tides <0.05 m/s 0.3–0.6 m/s 0.8–1.5 m/s
Stokes 0.02–0.05 m/s 0.02–0.04 m/s <0.02 m/s
Windage (3% of 8 m/s) ~0.24 m/s ~0.24 m/s ~0.24 m/s

For an open-ocean trajectory: currents and windage dominate, Stokes is a correction, tides are noise.

For a delta trajectory: tides dominate. Currents are a steady drift. Stokes and windage are corrections to the second decimal.

Run the same release point with and without each forcing — the diagnostic plot you get is the most informative single image you can make of how each term affects the answer.

8.6 Summary

  • Stokes drift is wave-induced second-order mean flow; small but not negligible.
  • Tides are the dominant signal in shelf/delta regions; CMEMS does not contain them.
  • Windage is direct wind drag on the emergent fraction of the object; small coefficient \(\times\) large wind speed = a real chunk of the drift.
  • DriftScope adds all three at the data layer — they sum into one combined velocity NetCDF that the integrator sees as a single field.
