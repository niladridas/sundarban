19  Tutorial 4 — The dynamics snapshot

20 Tutorial 4 — The dynamics snapshot

Goal: reproduce the four-panel dynamics figure inspired by Lenain et al. (2026)’s GOFLOW. Walks through what each panel shows, what the resolution limits mean, and how to interpret the result.

20.1 What we’re trying to make

Four panels, in a 2×2 grid:

  • (a) Surface velocity vectors over SST.
  • (b) \(\log_{10}|\nabla T|\) — the front map.
  • (c) \(\zeta / f\) — relative vorticity over Coriolis.
  • (d) \(\delta / f\) — divergence over Coriolis.

The structure is from a GOFLOW figure (Gulf Stream, sub-km resolution GOES-derived currents). We adapt it to HYCOM 1/25° currents + OSTIA L4 SST in the Bay of Bengal.

20.2 Step 1 — fetch the data

Pick a wider basin than the Sundarbans default; the snapshot needs open ocean to show eddy structure. We use the GBM pre-monsoon window:

# HYCOM (1/25°) for the open BoB
python -m driftscope.hycom \
    --bbox 87.5,19,92.5,23 \
    --start 2025-04-20 --end 2025-06-01 \
    --region "GBM premonsoon"

# OSTIA SST for the same window
python -m driftscope.sst \
    --bbox 87.5,19,92.5,23 \
    --start 2025-04-20 --end 2025-05-31

(43 days HYCOM ≈ 5 MB; 42 days OSTIA ≈ 700 KB.)

20.3 Step 2 — render the snapshot

The script scripts/sundarban_dynamics_snapshot.py does all four panels with one call:

python scripts/sundarban_dynamics_snapshot.py \
    --currents data/currents/gbm_premonsoon_2025-04-20_2025-06-01_hycom.nc \
    --sst data/sst/custom_2025-04-20_2025-05-31_sst.nc \
    --time-index 21 \
    --ref-lon 89.5 --ref-lat 20.5 \
    --quiver-stride 3 \
    --out data/sundarbans_dynamics_snapshot_wide.png

What the flags do:

Flag Meaning
--time-index Which day in the window (21 ≈ middle of run)
--ref-lon, --ref-lat Reference point for the inset speed time series
--quiver-stride Show every Nth velocity vector (3 = every third grid cell)
--out PNG output path

20.4 Step 3 — what the panels mean

20.4.1 Panel (a): velocity over SST

Two fields on one plot. The colormap shows \(T_\text{SST}\) in °C; the arrows show \((u, v)\) vectors at every third grid cell.

Read it as: the SST field is the thermal context. Cold cores = cyclonic eddies (upwelled subsurface water). Warm cores = anticyclonic. Sharp linear boundaries between warm and cold regions are fronts — typically high-shear, biologically productive, and where particle convergence happens.

The velocity vectors should wrap around SST eddies (a cyclonic eddy has counterclockwise flow in the NH) and cross fronts perpendicularly where the flow advects mass through them.

Tip

The inset. A small subplot in the upper right of (a) shows the velocity speed time series at the reference point over the analysis window. Daily-mean HYCOM filters out inertial oscillations (which have ~32 h period at 22°N) — the daily series tells you about synoptic-scale variability (1–2 weeks). For inertial-band signal you’d need hourly currents and a different plot.

20.4.2 Panel (b): log|∇T| — fronts

We compute the magnitude of the SST gradient at every cell:

\[ |\nabla T| = \sqrt{(\partial T/\partial x)^2 + (\partial T/\partial y)^2} \]

…and plot \(\log_{10}|\nabla T|\) in K/m. Why log: SST gradients span 3+ orders of magnitude (open ocean is smooth; fronts are sharp). Log scaling makes the full range readable in one image.

OSTIA at 5 km resolves a lot of front structure. Open Bay of Bengal during pre-monsoon shows:

  • Cool SST cap in the NW (mainland river outflows + cooler shelf water).
  • Warm tongue along the SE Andaman side.
  • Filaments wrapping eddies at \(|\nabla T| \sim 10^{-4}\) K/m (\(\log = -4\)).

The brightness in panel (b) is the best resolved view of the ocean’s small-scale structure that DriftScope has — even though the velocity model can’t resolve those filaments.

20.4.3 Panel (c): ζ/f — vorticity

From Chapter 7, \(\zeta / f\) measures the local “spin-iness” of the flow relative to background rotation:

\[ \zeta = \partial v / \partial x - \partial u / \partial y, \qquad f = 2 \Omega \sin\varphi \]

Plotted on a \(\pm 1\) red-blue colormap: red = positive \(\zeta\) (cyclonic, counterclockwise NH); blue = negative (anticyclonic).

In the wide-bbox snapshot, the dominant feature is a cyclonic eddy near 89.5°E / 22°N with \(\zeta/f \approx +0.5\) at its core. This is the same eddy visible as a cool patch in panel (a) and a wrap of \(|\nabla T|\) filaments in panel (b). When all four panels show the same feature in different ways, you’re looking at a real eddy.

20.4.4 Panel (d): δ/f — divergence

\[ \delta = \partial u / \partial x + \partial v / \partial y \]

Where \(\delta > 0\), water is diverging — flow exits the cell faster than it enters. By mass conservation, this requires upwelling (water coming from below to fill the surface deficit). \(\delta < 0\) implies convergence and downwelling.

Divergence is much smaller than vorticity in geostrophic flow (where incompressibility forces \(\delta \approx 0\)). Non-geostrophic features (fronts, filaments, ageostrophic eddies) light up here.

DriftScope’s HYCOM at 4 km strains hardest in this panel: \(\delta/f\) ranges \(\pm 0.2\), much noisier-looking than the cleaner \(\zeta/f\) field. This is the model’s resolution edge — what’s signal and what’s discretization noise gets harder to tell.

20.5 Step 4 — resolution caveat

Warning

The figure inspired this book because it shows something the model doesn’t see. The original GOFLOW panels show submesoscale filaments at sub-kilometer resolution. HYCOM at 4 km gives a macro version — the same eddies, smoothed. OSTIA SST at 5 km gives sharp fronts; HYCOM velocities at 4 km give blurry vorticity.

This mismatch is itself a useful figure. Side-by-side, panels (b) and (c) tell you “the ocean is sharper than the velocity model thinks”. For the full GOFLOW-style sub-km picture in the BoB, you’d need either a regional ROMS/CROCO run or future SWOT/GOFLOW-style products that cover that region.

20.6 Step 5 — variations

A few useful variants:

# Different day in the window
python scripts/sundarban_dynamics_snapshot.py \
    --time-index 5      # day 5 — earlier in pre-monsoon

# Smaller bbox (Sundarbans)
python scripts/sundarban_dynamics_snapshot.py \
    --currents data/currents/sundarbans_delta_2026-04-17_2026-05-01_hycom.nc \
    --sst data/sst/sundarbans_delta_2026-04-17_2026-05-01_sst.nc \
    --time-index 7

# Animate over the full window
for i in $(seq 0 42); do
    python scripts/sundarban_dynamics_snapshot.py \
        --time-index $i \
        --out data/dynamics_$(printf '%03d' $i).png
done
ffmpeg -framerate 4 -i data/dynamics_%03d.png \
    -c:v libx264 -pix_fmt yuv420p data/dynamics_animation.mp4

The animation makes the eddy life cycle visible: spin-up, mature, detach, dissipate. Static snapshots miss the time evolution.

20.7 Step 6 — in the bigger picture

This figure is not a particle simulation. It’s an Eulerian diagnostic of the velocity field that drives the simulation. You’d make one of these for every region/season you simulate, before running the trajectory model — to know what features the model resolves (visible eddies in \(\zeta/f\)) and what it doesn’t (sharp fronts in \(|\nabla T|\) that don’t show up in \(\zeta/f\)).

In a paper or report:

  • “We simulated 10-day drift with DriftScope. The dominant velocity feature in the simulation period was a cyclonic eddy at ~89°E (panel c, \(\zeta/f \approx 0.5\)) co-located with a 1°C cool SST anomaly (panel a). Particles released within the eddy rotated 1.5 cycles before exiting; particles outside drifted SW under the residual monsoon flow.”

That paragraph is impossible to write without the dynamics snapshot. With it, the simulation has physical context.

20.8 Reusable diagnostics

The math used in the script — gradient → curl/divergence → normalize by Coriolis — is also exposed as a single helper in driftscope/diagnostics.py:22:

from driftscope.diagnostics import compute_diagnostics, plot_diagnostics

d = compute_diagnostics(u, v, lats, lons)
print(d.keys())  # dict_keys(['speed', 'vorticity', 'divergence',
                 #            'grad_mag', 'rossby_vort', 'rossby_div', 'f'])
fig = plot_diagnostics(u, v, lats, lons, time_str="2025-05-10")

Use the helpers when you want the diagnostic fields for further analysis (FFTs, ensemble statistics). Use the script when you want the panel figure.

20.9 Summary

  • Four panels reveal complementary aspects of the surface flow: vectors over SST, fronts, vorticity, divergence.
  • Computed at the model’s native grid with simple finite differences and the Coriolis parameter at the local latitude.
  • Resolution-limited: 4 km HYCOM cannot resolve the submesoscale filaments visible in 5 km OSTIA SST. The mismatch is informative.
  • One command produces it; reusable helpers in diagnostics.py for custom analyses.

The final tutorial puts it all together — a seasonal-comparison run that exercises every module in this book.

Lenain, L. et al. (2026). GOFLOW: Geostationary ocean flow. Nature Geoscience. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41561-026-01943-0
