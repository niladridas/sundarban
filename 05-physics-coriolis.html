6  Coriolis and the inertial period

7 Coriolis and the inertial period

We’re on a rotating planet. Anything moving freely in a horizontal plane feels a sideways pseudo-force. This chapter explains the Coriolis parameter \(f\), the inertial period \(T_i = 2\pi / f\), and where they show up in the DriftScope diagnostics.

7.1 Why a sideways force shows up

In a non-rotating frame, Newton’s second law is \(\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}\). In a frame rotating at angular velocity \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\), the equation gains two pseudo-forces:

\[ m\,\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt} = \mathbf{F} - 2m\,\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{u} - m\,\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{r}) \tag{7.1}\]

The first new term is the Coriolis force, the second is centrifugal. For ocean dynamics on Earth, centrifugal is absorbed into apparent gravity (it just shifts what we call “down”). What remains, projected onto a horizontal plane at latitude \(\varphi\), is

\[ \frac{du}{dt} = +f\,v, \qquad \frac{dv}{dt} = -f\,u \tag{7.2}\]

with the Coriolis parameter

\[ f \;=\; 2\Omega \sin\varphi \;\approx\; 1.458\times 10^{-4}\,\sin\varphi \quad [\text{s}^{-1}] \tag{7.3}\]

where \(\Omega = 7.2921\times 10^{-5}\) rad/s is Earth’s angular velocity. Sign matters: \(f\) is positive in the Northern Hemisphere, negative in the Southern, zero on the equator.

Tip

Where does the factor of 2 come from? It’s the Coriolis \(-2\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{u}\) projected onto the horizontal — only the vertical component of \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) (\(= \Omega \sin\varphi\)) acts on horizontal motion. The factor 2 falls out of the cross-product algebra. See Phillips (1977) §1.5 for the derivation.

7.2 The inertial oscillation

Suppose nothing else acts on a parcel of water — no pressure gradient, no friction, no wind. Just Coriolis. Then Eq. 7.2 is a coupled linear ODE with eigenvalues \(\pm if\). The solution is

\[ u(t) = U_0 \cos(ft) + V_0 \sin(ft), \qquad v(t) = -U_0 \sin(ft) + V_0 \cos(ft) \]

A circle of radius \(|U|/f\), traced clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere (anticlockwise in the South), with period

\[ T_i \;=\; \frac{2\pi}{|f|} \tag{7.4}\]

Plug in the Sundarbans latitude \(\varphi = 22°\text{N}\):

\[ f \approx 5.46\times 10^{-5}\,\text{s}^{-1}, \qquad T_i \approx 32.0 \text{ h} \]

A patch of water given a kick will trace a roughly 32-hour circle and return near where it started. Real ocean trajectories, of course, don’t do this cleanly — there’s always a pressure gradient, friction, etc. — but the inertial period is a fundamental timescale of the system.

Note

The Bay of Bengal has a particularly clear inertial signal: it sits in the trade-wind belt, gets episodic monsoon “kicks”, and has weak background flow over much of its central basin. Drifter spectra often show a clean peak at \(T_i \approx 32\) h. CMEMS daily means hide this peak (daily averaging is a low-pass filter that kills 32-h signals) — which is why DriftScope’s currents-only validation against drifters tends to underestimate short-time scatter.

7.3 \(f\) in the codebase

The Coriolis parameter shows up explicitly only in the diagnostics module — the integrator doesn’t need it because it’s already implicit in any Eulerian velocity field that obeys the equations of motion. From driftscope/diagnostics.py:18:

OMEGA = 7.2921e-5        # rad/s
...
f = 2 * OMEGA * np.sin(np.deg2rad(LAT2D))

It’s used in two normalized diagnostics:

\[ \frac{\zeta}{f} = \frac{\partial v / \partial x - \partial u / \partial y}{f}, \qquad \frac{\delta}{f} = \frac{\partial u / \partial x + \partial v / \partial y}{f} \tag{7.5}\]

The first is relative vorticity (rotation of the flow at a point) over \(f\). The second is divergence over \(f\). Both are dimensionless. Both are flavors of Rossby number — measures of how strongly the flow deviates from geostrophic balance.

\(\zeta / f\) What it means
\(\ll 1\) Geostrophic flow; rotation negligible compared to background spin
\(\sim 0.5\) Strong eddies, frontal regions
\(\gtrsim 1\) Submesoscale dynamics; cyclostrophic balance
Very high The model is probably resolving features it shouldn’t

A real submesoscale-resolving product (GOFLOW, the figure that started this book) shows \(\zeta / f\) filaments stronger than \(\pm 1\). CMEMS at 1/12° and HYCOM at 1/25° barely reach \(\pm 0.5\), even at the strongest eddy edges — they smooth over the dynamics that produce \(|\zeta/f| > 1\).

7.4 What this looks like in DriftScope

The script scripts/sundarban_dynamics_snapshot.py produces a four-panel figure (the structure inspired by Lenain et al. (2026)) that puts \(\zeta/f\) and \(\delta/f\) on the same page as the velocity field and the SST gradient. The dynamics chapter (Chapter 20) walks through this end-to-end. For now, the punchline:

  1. Panel (a): \(\mathbf{u}\) vectors over SST. Shows the cyclonic eddy.
  2. Panel (b): \(\log_{10}|\nabla T|\). Sharp filaments visible at OSTIA’s ~5 km resolution.
  3. Panel (c): \(\zeta/f\). The eddy lights up at \(+0.5\).
  4. Panel (d): \(\delta/f\). Fronts and convergences, ±0.2.

The figure is reproducible with one command:

python scripts/sundarban_dynamics_snapshot.py \
  --currents data/currents/gbm_premonsoon_2025-04-20_2025-06-01_hycom.nc \
  --sst data/sst/custom_2025-04-20_2025-05-31_sst.nc \
  --time-index 21
Warning

Resolution caveat. \(\zeta/f\) and \(\delta/f\) as computed by DriftScope report what the grid sees, not what the ocean does. HYCOM at 4 km captures the mesoscale but smears the submesoscale. A patient student can compute the same diagnostics from a real GDP drifter trajectory using position differencing — the field-vs-trajectory comparison is one of the most useful exercises in this book; see Chapter 19.

7.5 \(\beta\) and what we’re ignoring

Strictly, \(f\) varies with latitude: \(f = f_0 + \beta y\) where \(\beta = df/dy\). This \(\beta\)-effect drives Rossby waves and the western intensification of boundary currents. Over the few-degrees latitude range of a typical DriftScope run, \(\beta y \ll f_0\) and the constant-\(f\) approximation is fine for a single trajectory. Over basin-scale runs (full Bay of Bengal, months) it matters; CMEMS already includes it implicitly because the model that produced CMEMS solves the full equations.

A reader who wants to push this further should read Phillips (1977) chapters 3–4 for the wave dynamics, then Geyer & MacCready (2014) for the estuarine context.

7.6 Summary

  • Coriolis is a frame-of-reference effect; on a rotating planet, horizontal motion couples through Eq. 7.2.
  • The inertial period \(T_i = 2\pi/f\) is a fundamental ocean timescale; \(\approx 32\) h at Sundarbans, \(\approx 17\) h at 45° latitude.
  • \(f\) shows up in DriftScope only in diagnostics, where \(\zeta/f\) and \(\delta/f\) measure how strongly the flow deviates from geostrophic balance.
  • Daily-mean currents (CMEMS, HYCOM) filter out inertial oscillations. The drifter-validation chapter shows what gets lost.
